"High-risk" is an insurance industry term, not a permanent label — and it's more common than most drivers assume. Here's what it means in Florida and how to navigate it.

## What makes a driver high-risk

Insurers typically classify a driver as high-risk based on factors like DUI/DWI convictions, multiple at-fault accidents, a pattern of moving violations, driving without insurance, or being a newly licensed driver with limited history. Any of these can lead to higher premiums or a requirement for FR44 or SR22 filings.

## FR44 and SR22: the two main filings

In Florida, DUI-related convictions typically require an FR44 filing, with liability limits above the state's standard minimums. Other violations, like driving without insurance, more commonly require an SR22 filing at standard limits. Both are certificates your insurer files with the FLHSMV — see our [FR44 vs. SR22 guide](/guides/fr44-vs-sr22/) for the full comparison.

## Not every carrier writes high-risk policies

Some standard insurers decline to write policies for high-risk drivers at all, while others specialize in this category and often price it more competitively. This is one of the biggest reasons comparison shopping matters more for high-risk coverage than for standard policies — the price spread between carriers tends to be wider.

## What it costs

High-risk premiums are generally higher than standard rates, reflecting the increased claims risk insurers associate with the driver's history. The exact increase depends heavily on the specific violation, your overall driving record, and which carrier you choose.

## How to find affordable high-risk coverage

- Compare multiple carriers, prioritizing those that specialize in FR44/SR22 and high-risk policies
- Maintain continuous coverage without lapses
- Ask about every applicable discount — defensive driving courses, paid-in-full, and bundling can all help
- Keep your record clean going forward to work toward standard-risk pricing over time

## How long "high-risk" status lasts

This depends on the underlying reason. FR44 and SR22 filing requirements typically last three years. Beyond that, how long a specific violation continues to affect your rate varies by carrier — some weigh older incidents much less than recent ones, though the violation itself may remain on your driving record far longer.

## Getting back to standard coverage

Once your required filing period ends and your record stays clean, you'll typically qualify for standard-risk policies again, often at meaningfully better rates than during your high-risk period.
